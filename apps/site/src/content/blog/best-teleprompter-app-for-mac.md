> Judge a Mac teleprompter on five things: whether it tracks your words or just runs a motor, whether it hides from screen capture, how many clicks it takes to start, which languages it recognizes, and whether the price model fits how often you record. We make gotoprompter, so read our pick with that in mind.

## What matters

"Best" lists for teleprompters tend to rank features that look good on a comparison grid, such as the number of fonts, and skip the ones that decide whether you look natural on camera. After building one and using many, we think five criteria cover almost every real decision. We have put them in the order they matter to us.

## Voice tracking

The first question is how the text moves. There are three answers, and they are not interchangeable.

A **constant-speed** prompter runs a motor. You read at its pace. It is fine for broadcast-style reading from a locked script and exhausting for anything conversational.

A **sound-activated** prompter moves while the microphone hears noise. It stops when you stop, which is better, but it cannot tell whether you are reading, ad-libbing, or answering a question.

A **word-tracking** prompter transcribes your speech and matches it against the script. Done well, it waits while you improvise, finds you when you skip ahead, and backs up when you repeat a line. Done badly, it jumps to the wrong place on a common word. The test is simple and takes a minute: pause, ad-lib, skip two paragraphs, repeat a sentence. We described it in detail in [[how-a-voice-following-teleprompter-works]].

If you talk to a camera rather than read a news bulletin, this criterion outweighs all the others.

## Hidden from capture

If you present over Zoom, Meet or Teams, or record your screen, the second question is whether other people can see the prompter.

macOS lets an app exclude its window from screen capture. Apps that do this are invisible in every share and recording while staying visible to you. Apps that do not rely on workarounds: a second monitor, sharing a single window, or hoping nobody notices. Check that the setting exists, that it is on by default, and then verify it yourself by sharing your screen in a test meeting.

A related point is whether the window stays on top of full-screen apps. A prompter that disappears behind a full-screen Keynote deck is a prompter you will not use for presentations.

## Start friction

Count the steps between "I have a script" and "the text is on screen and listening". The best case is two: copy the text, click one menu-bar item. The worst case involves creating a project, naming it, importing a file, choosing a template and pressing play.

Friction decides whether you reach for the tool for a two-minute Loom or only for a planned shoot. Also look for a recent-scripts list, because you will re-record the same intro more often than you expect.

## Languages

If you ever present in more than one language, check two things. First, which languages the recognizer supports; on-device Apple recognition covers more than sixty. Second, whether the app detects the language from the script or makes you pick it from a menu every time. Mixed-language scripts are a bonus: being able to bracket a foreign phrase so it is shown but not matched keeps the sync intact.

## Price models

Teleprompter usage is bursty, so the price model matters more than the price. The honest options are a free tier that is genuinely useful, a pay-once licence with clear update terms, and a subscription for people who prefer a smaller yearly amount. Be wary of trials that convert silently and of "free" apps whose one essential feature is paid. We wrote more in [[teleprompter-without-subscription]].

Finally, ask where your scripts are stored and what the app sends over the network. A desktop prompter has no reason to upload your text.
